OpenAI says an unreleased model, "Astra," produced ten new results in mathematics and theoretical computer science

2026-08-01 models research openai mathematics

OpenAI said an internal, unreleased version of its next model family — tentatively code-named Astra, which Sam Altman has been previewing to lawmakers and Trump administration officials in Washington this past week — produced new results on ten previously open problems spanning group theory, sphere packing, complexity theory, and extremal combinatorics. Highlights include the first explicit construction of a non-sofic group, a question open since Mikhail Gromov introduced the concept in 1999, a disproof of Connes's rigidity conjecture, and solutions to three problems from Paul Erdős's catalogue. Human mathematicians turned Astra's arguments into full manuscripts, and the model then formalized each proof as a machine-checked Lean certificate, all published on GitHub; OpenAI put the total inference cost across all ten proofs at roughly $2,000. Astra itself hasn't launched, and OpenAI hasn't decided whether to release it as GPT-6 or a GPT-5-family model.

Why it matters

If the results hold up under mathematician scrutiny, it's one of the clearest demonstrations yet of a language model producing genuinely new, independently verifiable research mathematics rather than recombining known results.

Trump's AI executive order misses its first major deadline

2026-08-01 policy regulation us-government safety

The 60-day clock set by President Trump's June 2 executive order on frontier AI and cybersecurity (EO 14409) expired August 1 without the government publishing any of its three required deliverables: a classified NSA/CISA/NIST process for benchmarking which AI systems count as "covered frontier models," a voluntary pre-release disclosure framework for developers, and a federal cyber-workforce expansion plan. As of the deadline there was no Federal Register notice, no NIST or CISA publication, and no statement from the Office of Science and Technology Policy. The order's one confirmed deliverable so far is GOLD EAGLE, an AI-focused cybersecurity vulnerability-coordination clearinghouse the White House launched July 14. Note this was a design deadline for the government, not a compliance deadline for AI companies — nothing becomes binding on developers today.

Why it matters

A missed self-imposed deadline on the administration's own framework for reviewing powerful AI systems before release raises questions about how quickly Washington can stand up the oversight machinery it says it wants, even as it resists mandatory pre-release rules.

Update: EU begins actively enforcing AI Act transparency rules for chatbots and deepfakes

2026-08-02 policy eu regulation transparency

What's new since we last reported: the AI Act Article 50 transparency guidelines the European Commission finalized on July 21 took effect today, with the Commission's AI Office and national authorities now formally enforcing them rather than just having published the rules. Chatbots and other interactive AI systems must now disclose that users are dealing with AI rather than a human; deepfakes — AI-generated or altered images, video, or audio resembling real people — must be labeled regardless of any intent to deceive; and AI-generated content must carry machine-readable marks so it can be detected. Non-compliance can draw fines of up to €15 million or 3% of global annual turnover.

Why it matters

It's one of the first AI Act provisions with real teeth to move from published guidance to active enforcement, making the EU the first major jurisdiction to require broad, system-level AI disclosure to end users.
